Quantitative Trading Backtester

A Python framework that takes a stream of trading signals (with optional Take-Profit and Stop-Loss levels) and a master OHLCV dataset, simulates the trades bar-by-bar, and reports 18+ metrics covering profitability, risk, and consistency. Built for the Summer of Quant cohort and used in a strategy that placed in the Top 10.

Summer 2025 Python · pandas · Plotly BTC daily, 2019–2023

What it does

Input is two CSVs: signals (timestamp, direction, optional TP/SL) and a master OHLCV reference — for this project, daily Bitcoin candles from Sept 2019 through 2023. The BackTester class walks the signal stream, opens and closes Position objects, checks for intra-bar TP/SL triggers, and collects each completed round-trip as a TradePair. From those it computes everything from net PnL to max drawdown to consecutive-loss streaks.

The interesting parts

1. Intra-bar TP/SL execution

Most beginner backtests fill at the candle close, which silently inflates results because TP and SL targets are often hit somewhere inside the bar, not at its end. check_tp_sl() walks the master data within each bar and closes the position at the actual trigger price — high ≥ TP for longs, low ≤ TP for shorts. That single change is the difference between a backtest that looks great and one that's actually defensible.

2. Position-reversal signals

The signal vocabulary supports values ±1 (open) and ±2 (close-and-reverse), so a single bar can flip from a long to a short atomically. This avoids the gap-risk ambiguity of issuing a close followed by a new open across two bars.

3. Lookahead-bias check

Before trusting any signal, main.py re-runs the strategy on progressively truncated data and flags any signal that wouldn't have fired given only the information available at that time. ATR(14) at bar i is computed only on bars [:i], so no future data leaks back into the decision — easy to forget, painful to debug after the fact.

4. Realistic costs

Every trade pays a 0.15% transaction cost in the PnL formula. Small per-trade, big impact on the Sharpe number — and a reminder that "this strategy works" usually means "this strategy works after realistic frictions."

Strategy on top

The bundled strat() in main.py is a volume-spike trend follower: a long entry on a 1.5σ-above-mean volume spike with a green close (short on red), trailing stop at close ± 2×ATR, exit on three consecutive adverse closes or a stop breach. Nothing exotic — the backtester was the point.

Metrics reported

  • Net Profit, Win Rate, Largest Win / Loss
  • Sharpe Ratio (annualised), Max & Average Drawdown
  • Max / Average Holding Time, Consecutive Win/Loss Streaks
  • Benchmark return (buy-and-hold)

Visualised with Plotly: a candlestick chart with green/red trade regions, and a dual-axis plot of capital evolution against the underlying price.

What I'd carry forward

The lesson wasn't the strategy — it was that most of what makes a backtest trustworthy is friction: intra-bar fills, realistic costs, lookahead checks. Without those, every chart slopes up and to the right.
