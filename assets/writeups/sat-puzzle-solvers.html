SAT-Based Puzzle Solvers

Two puzzles reduced to Boolean satisfiability and solved with off-the-shelf SAT solvers via PySAT: Sudoku (9×9) and Sokoban (parameterised grid). The exercise is in the encoding — once the puzzle is a CNF formula, the solver does all the search.

IIT Bombay · Autumn 2025 Python · PySAT (Glucose3 / g3) Pair assignment

Q1 — Sudoku as SAT

The 9×9 board needs 729 Boolean variables — one per (row, col, digit) triple — with the mapping id = 100·i + 10·j + k for readability. From there the constraints write themselves:

  • Cell uniqueness — each cell holds exactly one digit.
  • Row, column, and 3×3 subgrid — each digit appears at least once and at most once in each.

Strictly, the "at most one" clauses are logically redundant — they're implied by "at least one" plus the cell-uniqueness constraint via pigeonhole. They were added anyway because helping the solver beats being elegant: redundant constraints prune the search tree faster. Solved with Solver(name='glucose3').

Q2 — Sokoban as SAT

Sokoban is a planning problem: push boxes onto goal tiles without pulling, without squeezing into corners. Encoding it requires time as a dimension.

State variables are time-indexed: var_player(x, y, t) and var_box(b, x, y, t). The plan horizon T is fixed up front and the encoder emits six families of clauses:

  • Initial state — player and boxes start at their given cells.
  • Player movement — at step t, the player moves to an adjacent free cell, with action-precondition constraints.
  • Box pushing — a box moves only when the player walks into it and the cell behind is free.
  • Non-overlap — no two boxes in one cell; no player/box/wall overlap.
  • Goal conditions — every goal tile holds a box at step T.
  • Uniqueness / existence — the player is in exactly one cell at each step; each box has exactly one position.

Solved with Solver(name='g3'). The interesting engineering was the variable-ID arithmetic — assigning each (entity, position, time) tuple a globally unique positive integer without collisions across the player and box variable spaces.

What I'd carry forward

Two lessons. First: declarative encoding makes the essence of the problem visible in a way procedural search doesn't — every constraint is named, and the solver tells you when you've under-specified the problem. Second: redundant clauses that look like noise to a human are often load-bearing for the solver. Logical minimality and search-tree minimality point in different directions.
